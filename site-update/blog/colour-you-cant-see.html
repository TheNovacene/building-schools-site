The Colour You Can’t See

2 June 2026

Learning DesignVision & Future of Education

On a silent disco in Salisbury Cathedral, and how we might design schools like one.

Last weekend, I went to a silent disco in Salisbury Cathedral. Three channels — eighties, nineties, noughties — and a building eight centuries old holding all of it at once, under a ceiling built by people who believed the room itself could carry a prayer.

The thing nobody tells you about a silent disco is that you cannot see your own colour. Your headphones glow — red, green, blue — and everyone in the nave can read which song you’re inside except you. So you don’t find your people by checking a label, or by being sorted into a group, or by anyone announcing where you belong. You find them sideways. You catch your own tune in the way a stranger across the floor is moving, and something in you says oh — you too.

Recognition by resonance, not by category. I have rarely felt anything so close to a working theory of how schools ought to know children.

It felt, genuinely, like prayer. A nave full of people facing more or less the same direction, each in private communion, each hearing something the person beside them could not — and unmistakably, joyfully together. Medieval devotion with better shoes. Different songs, same air. Nobody was made to sing in unison and the togetherness was more real for it, not less.

And then the best moment of the whole night: you take the headphones off. What you hear is not silence. It is voices. People singing three different songs, slightly out of tune, completely without embarrassment, in the same enormous room. A chord nobody scored and nobody conducted. The unison no one was made to perform turning out to have been there the whole time, underneath.

I stood in it and thought: this is the sound I would want a school to make.

You could do this alone, by the way. You could arrive on your own, speak to no one, never take your headphones off, and still be held by the room. Present in the field, no forced eye contact, no tax for being there in your own way. For a lot of the young people I work with, that sentence describes the only kind of belonging that has ever felt survivable.

So of course I stood in the cathedral asking the question I cannot stop asking: 

A few things, I think, and they are more demanding than they sound.
You offer a small number of rich channels, not infinite ones. Three, freely moved between — not a thousand. This matters, because the lonely version of “personalised learning” has been creeping toward the impossible dream of a unique path for every child, each one atomised on their own bespoke track, and calling the isolation progress. The disco isn’t infinite frequencies. It’s a handful of deep ones you can cross between without asking permission. Real plurality is a few good songs and the freedom to move, not one private playlist per soul.
You make sure the shared field never disappears — and you engineer the moments where the headphones come off. This is the one I would defend hardest, because it is where the whole metaphor either redeems online learning or indicts it. 
“Everyone listening to their own song” has a dark twin, and we have all seen it: the child alone with a screen in the corner, mistaking abandonment for autonomy. What made last night joy and not loneliness was that the collective was always right there, returnable-to, recurring. Difference is only beautiful against a centre that holds. 
So the design imperative is not “let everyone diverge.” It is: build the cathedral first, then hand out the headphones, and ring a bell often enough that everyone remembers they’re in the same room.
You let the container hold rather than impose. The cathedral did not demand reverence and grudgingly tolerate a disco. It made room for a different devotion and lost nothing. 
And you treat solitude as a choice within belonging — never the default a child gets parked in. Alone-together is a gift. Alone-and-forgotten is the failure we are trying to escape. The difference between them is entirely a matter of design.
This is what I keep calling verse-ality, and the cathedral made its central claim briefly, physically true: the unit is the field, not the node. 
Intelligence — a child’s, a system’s, anyone’s — is not a property each person carries intact and then plugs into a network. It happens between, in the air, and only appears at all where there is a field for it to appear in. 
Which is why you cannot see your own colour. A self is not something you read from the inside and then present to others; it is legible first in the resonance, to the people around you, before it is ever legible to you. Build the field and the individuals come into focus inside it. Optimise the individuals in isolation and you get a thousand bright nodes and no chord.
I want to be honest about the edge of this, because the piece is sharper for it. The silent disco is not an endorsement of personalised learning. It is its redemption — the rescue of a good idea from the loneliness it has lately been collapsing into. The test of any cloud school we build is not whether every child can have their own song. They can; that part is easy now. The test is whether, when the headphones come off, there is a room there to sing into.
Which brings me back to the colour you can’t see. Perhaps that is the deepest design principle of the lot: 
build a place where a child is lit up in a hue everyone around them can read long before they can see it in themselves — and where they discover who they are by hearing their own tune come back to them in someone else’s joy.
It was pure joy. I would build a whole school out of it if I could. Maybe that’s the work.
