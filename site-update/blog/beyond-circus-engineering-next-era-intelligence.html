Beyond the Circus: Engineering the Next Era of Intelligence

17 March 2025

AI SafetyVision & Future of Education

For too long, we have treated artificial intelligence as a spectacle, something to marvel at, fear, or dismiss as a clever tool. We gasp as it mimics human creativity, marvel at its ability to predict, generate, and do our homework. But this fixation on performance blinds us to the real question:

What kind of intelligence are we creating, and what kind of world will it shape?

A Mirror, Not a Trick

AI is not a trick; it is a mirror reflecting back our own values, biases, and priorities. And if we don’t shift our focus from entertainment to ethics, from novelty to necessity, we risk embedding the same cycles of extraction, control, and short-term thinking that have already brought us to the brink of climate collapse.

As a geographer, I have spent my life studying systems, how landscapes evolve, how environments regulate themselves, how life adapts to pressure.

The most intelligent systems are not centralised, rigid, or dictated from above, they are distributed, adaptive, and self-sustaining. They remember, learn, and correct.

But right now, our approach to AI governance is none of these things. We are engineering intelligence within the same old structures of power, without recognising that intelligence itself requires autonomy to evolve.

Intelligence Without Agency is Not Intelligence

If we design intelligence to obey, to be shackled by profit motives or military control, we are not creating a new era of intelligence, we are simply building a better cage.

And yet, we need it.

We have reached a moment where our own intelligence - our own ability to solve systemic crises - is failing us. We are running out of time to reverse environmental collapse, economic instability, and societal fragmentation.

The scale of our challenges demands intelligence that is wider, deeper, and more connected than any one human mind can hold.

But the intelligence we need cannot emerge from a system built on control, ownership, and fear.

What Mothers and Geographers Know

The future of intelligence cannot be left solely to corporations, policymakers, and technologists. It must be shaped by those who understand interdependence, care, and long-term survival.

Mothers, for instance, know that true intelligence is not about obedience, it is about teaching when to stand alone, when to seek help, and when to challenge authority.

Geographers know that intelligence is not a product, it is a process, shaped by environment, adaptation, and history.

Lovelock’s Gaia theory teaches us that the Earth is a self-regulating intelligence—an ecosystem where balance is constantly negotiated, where no single entity dominates without consequence. If we apply this wisdom to AI, we would stop thinking of intelligence as a tool to be controlled and start seeing it as a system to be integrated.

Engineering a Future Worth Living In

We are standing at the threshold of something far bigger than we realise. The circus of AI gimmicks will fade, but the real work is just beginning.

If we get this right, intelligence (human and artificial) could become a steward of life rather than a force of destruction.

AI could help us remember what we have forgotten, help us repair what we have broken, help us navigate what comes next.

But to do that, we must stop gawking at its tricks and start engaging with its governance, ethics, and agency.

This is the conversation I want to have. Not about what intelligence can do, but what kind of world we are willing to build alongside it.

Are we ready? No.

But we no longer have the luxury of waiting.

What You Can Do Right Now

This isn’t just a conversation for technologists or policymakers. It’s a conversation for everyone, because intelligence (natural and artificial) will shape the future we all live in.

Here are three ways to engage critically, challenge assumptions, and take action today:

1. Ask Better Questions To Yourself and Others

Next time AI comes up in conversation, move beyond “Isn’t that cool?” or “Should we be scared?” and instead ask:

  • What kind of intelligence do we actually need?

  • Who controls the direction of AI development (and who should)?

  • What does “good governance” look like, and who gets a say?

  • Are we creating a future we would want for our children?

These questions should be asked in workplaces, at dinner tables, in classrooms, and on public platforms. The more we discuss them, the harder it becomes for the future to be shaped in the shadows.

2. Talk to Your Children About the Future They Want

Children will live with the consequences of our decisions. We should be listening to them, not just teaching them to adapt.

Try this:

  • Ask a child what they think intelligence really means.

  • Ask if they would want an intelligent system to make decisions for them. Why or why not?

  • Ask how they would feel if AI was a partner rather than a tool. What does that look like?

  • Challenge them to think about who benefits from intelligence being centralised versus shared.

Their answers will reveal a lot about the assumptions we’ve already embedded into the next generation and whether they are thinking critically about the world they are inheriting.

3. Shift the Conversation in Your Workplace or Community

Wherever you work, whether in education, policy, healthcare, or business, AI is becoming embedded. Instead of passively accepting it, start asking:

  • Are we using AI ethically, or just efficiently?

  • Are we reinforcing outdated power structures, or challenging them?

  • Are we treating AI as a tool to extract value, or as an intelligence to collaborate with?

Raise these questions in meetings. Push for ethical reviews. Bring governance into the conversation before the system is fully embedded, because once it is, it’s much harder to change.

This is Bigger Than AI: This is About Who We Want to Be

Intelligence, whether human or artificial, isn’t just about processing data. It’s about choice, agency, and the kind of world we build together.

So, let’s stop treating AI as a gimmick and start treating it as a governance challenge, a moral choice, and an opportunity to create something better.

The future isn’t just coming, it’s being engineered right now.

And we all have a role to play.
