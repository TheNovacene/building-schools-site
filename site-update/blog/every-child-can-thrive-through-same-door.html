Not Every Child Can Thrive Through the Same Door

26 March 2026

Vision & Future of EducationSchool Models & Case Studies

A few days before this conversation, I took myself to Marrakesh. I needed sun, space, and a reminder that life can still contain rhythm, beauty and enough hours in the day to breathe. Walking through the city, I found myself photographing doors, different shapes, different colours, different thresholds. By the time I got home, I realised that was part of what had been troubling me all along: we are still trying to push far too many children through one narrow entrance and calling it education.

I didn't travel for work or for networking, not even for inspiration, at least not consciously. I went because parenting is hard, life has been hard, and there was a small five-day window in which I realised I could either keep pushing through or admit that I needed to stop.

So I left.

I stretched. I ran. I swam. I slept. I wrote a little. I wandered through markets thick with spice and colour. I drank mint tea poured extravagantly from silver teapots with the kind of care that makes you realise how starved modern life has made you for slowness. I let henna dry on my hands. I did no work. I didn’t talk to AI. I barely looked at a screen.

And somewhere between the quiet mornings and the sensory excess of the souk, I remembered something simple and almost shocking: there are enough hours in the day.

Enough hours to move your body. Enough hours to think. Enough hours to rest. Enough hours to notice things. Enough hours to be a person.

The problem is that many of us (and far too many of our children) are no longer living inside time that feels human.

We are living inside pressure.

When I got back, I had one of the most honest conversations I’ve had in a long time with another parent working in education. We talked, in anonymised but painfully recognisable terms, about teenage boys, social media, drugs, sleep collapse, emotional volatility, school pressure, and the sheer improvisation of trying to keep children safe in systems that often seem to make things worse rather than better.

And the truth is, I no longer think these are separate issues.

I think many young people are being shaped by a brutal stack: factory-model schooling, algorithmic addiction, poor sleep, chemical coping, social pressure, and an education system still pretending that all children can be made to thrive through the same narrow form.

That conversation left me with one overwhelming conviction:

we are breaking children, and then asking why they are struggling to bend back into shape.

Which is why I want to talk not only about what is going wrong, but about what might come next — and why hyflex education, done properly, may be one of the most hopeful paths we have.

This week I had one of those conversations that stays with you.

Not because it was polished. Not because it offered easy answers. But because it was honest.

Two parents. Two teenage boys. Different families, different contexts, but many of the same forces pressing in: social media, drugs, sleep collapse, school anxiety, emotional volatility, the impossible improvisation of parenting children through systems that were never really designed for the realities they are facing.

What struck me most was not the intensity of the stories, though there was plenty of that.

It was the clarity.

We are still far too willing to individualise what are, in many cases, systemic injuries.

We talk about “difficult teenagers”, “poor choices”, “lack of resilience”, “attention problems”, “behaviour issues”.

But what if some of what we are seeing is much simpler and much darker?

What if we are breaking children, then blaming them for the symptoms?

The stack is not neutral

When a young person is already navigating neurodivergence, anxiety, trauma, identity formation or simply the raw instability of adolescence, they are not meeting social media, substances, school pressure and algorithmic compulsion on a level playing field.

They are meeting them with a nervous system still under construction.

And that matters.

In our conversation, we found ourselves circling the same hard truth: sleep disruption, nicotine, weed, compulsive scrolling, stimulant misuse, emotional flooding and withdrawal are not isolated issues. They are entangled. They form a stack.

A child who is dysregulated does not make “bad decisions” in a vacuum. They reach for what alters state quickly. They look for relief. They look for belonging. They look for numbing. They look for something that works now.

And meanwhile many of our schools are still asking them to sit still, comply, mask, perform, revise, and demonstrate progress as though the wider ecology is irrelevant.

It isn’t irrelevant. It is the context.

The education model is part of the problem

One line from the conversation hit me in the chest: the sense that education, in its current factory form, is itself contributing to the harm.

I think many of us know this, but we are still too polite about saying it.

For some children, the traditional school day does not merely fail to help. It actively compounds dysregulation.

The pace. The noise. The social density. The performative demands. The punishment of inconsistency. The lack of real flexibility. The insistence that one route counts more than another.

And then, when children crack under the weight of it, we call the crack the problem.

Sometimes the problem is the mould.

Yet the answer is not to give up on education

This is where I feel hopeful.

Because the same conversation that named the damage also pointed towards something better.

Not fantasy. Not techno-utopian nonsense. Not “AI will fix it”.

Something more grounded than that.

Hyflex education.

Done well, hyflex is not just online schooling with better branding. It is not a cost-saving device. It is not a lesser substitute for “real school”.

It is a design principle.

It says:

  • learning can happen in more than one place

  • regulation matters

  • belonging can be built differently

  • some young people need more spaciousness, not more force

  • some need fewer transitions, fewer bodies, fewer sensory demands

  • some need online as the stable base from which life can slowly expand again

  • and many still need in-person community, but in smaller, safer, more intentional forms

That last part matters.

The future is not fully virtual. I don’t believe that.

But I also no longer believe the binary we have inherited: either children attend a conventional school building full-time, or they somehow fall outside “proper” education.

That binary is obsolete.

What I keep seeing in practice

I’ve now seen enough to say this plainly.

When education becomes more relational, more flexible and more intelligently designed around actual human nervous systems, children who were previously described as disengaged, anxious, non-attending, oppositional or impossible often begin to reappear.

Not overnight. Not by magic. Not without support.

But they reappear.

I’ve seen young people who could not cope with mainstream classrooms attend online consistently.

I’ve seen families exhale for the first time in years.

I’ve seen learners begin to rebuild confidence because the environment stopped attacking their regulation every day.

I’ve seen hybrid models work beautifully when they combine online learning with carefully chosen in-person experiences: meet-ups, projects, performances, practical workshops, city pods, field-based learning, small-group intensives.

In other words: human connection, but without the brutality of forcing every child through the same door in the same way.

We need broader definitions of success

Part of the trap is that we still treat one narrow educational path as the gold standard.

A particular set of subjects. A particular timetable. A particular trajectory. A particular kind of “achievement”.

But many young people do not need more pressure to conform to an idealised path. They need a path that can actually hold them.

That may include vocational routes, practical learning, place-based experiences, creative performance, ecological inquiry, alternative qualifications, apprenticeships, hybrid study, slower pacing, smaller communities, and explicit teaching around relational intelligence, self-regulation and technology.

It may also include recognising that some futures are not lesser because they are different.

A life with meaningful work, decent health, some peace, some autonomy and some joy is not a failed outcome just because it doesn’t look like a brochure.

So where does AI fit?

Not at the centre.

That is the first thing to say.

Children do not need more machine intimacy layered onto already destabilised nervous systems.

But they may need forms of education that are more adaptive, more personalised, more flexible in timing and mode, and better able to connect learning with the world they are actually entering.

That is where thoughtful technology can help.

Not by replacing adults. Not by replacing schools with platforms. Not by automating relationship. But by making more humane educational architectures possible.

Hyflex education, at its best, is not anti-human. It is anti-rigidity.

And for a growing number of young people, that distinction may be everything.

The question I’m left with

If we know that social media, substances, sleep dysregulation and systemic pressure are reshaping childhood, then why are we still designing education as though none of that is true?

And if we know some children are already showing us better ways to learn — through flexibility, distance, intensity, pacing, special interest, smaller groups, mixed modes and different rhythms of connection — why are we still treating that as marginal?

Maybe the task now is not to drag every child back into the old shape.

Maybe it is to build forms of school that are finally honest about the world they are growing up in.

That is the work.

Not school versus cloud.

But schools in the cloud, with roots in real life.

Perhaps the future of education begins when we stop insisting on one narrow entrance, and start building for the many doors children actually need.
