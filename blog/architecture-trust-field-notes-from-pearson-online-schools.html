The Architecture of Trust: Field Notes from Pearson Online Schools Day

1 July 2026

School Models & Case StudiesSystems & Strategy

Yesterday I spent the day with leaders from across the UK's online education sector. Long-established online schools sat alongside people building entirely new models. We talked about accreditation, AI, assessment, flexible schooling, safeguarding, teacher recruitment and the future of policy. There was research, optimism, disagreement and no shortage of ambition.

There was also something else.

Travelling home, I realised that although every presentation had a different title, they were all quietly asking the same question.

How do we build trust when school is no longer defined by a building?

For years, online education has defended its own existence. Can children really learn online? Can they socialise, build relationships, be known well by their teachers? Increasingly, those weren't the questions in the room. The conversation has matured.

Nobody was presenting emergency remote learning. Nobody was arguing that online should replace every school. Instead, people were sharing the craft of designing belonging at a distance.

Speakers described the challenge as breaking the fourth wall: creating genuine human connection through a camera lens. Another spoke about building virtual corridors alongside virtual classrooms. We heard about recruiting teachers not simply for subject expertise but for kindness, reflection and integrity, and about why peer coaching often matters more than technical training.

Different sessions, one underlying idea: relationships are no longer the thing that happens around learning. They are the learning infrastructure itself.

The same pattern surfaced in the discussions about artificial intelligence. Much has been written about cheating; much less about trust. One of the most refreshing ideas of the day was that AI policy should be a trellis rather than a cage. Rather than endlessly trying to detect AI use, schools are beginning to redesign assessment so that learning becomes visible through dialogue, process and reflection.

That feels like a healthier question. Not can we catch students using AI, but how do we design learning that values thinking over performance?

Technology hasn't removed the need for relationships. If anything, it has made them more valuable.

The moment that resonated most came during a discussion on flexible schooling. One slide simply listed pairs of phrases.

Education, or school attendance?

Flexibility, or unorthodox attendance?

Flexischooling, or part-time schooling?

Children not in school?

At first glance, semantic differences. I don't think they are.

Language shapes policy. Policy shapes culture. Culture shapes children's lives.

Describe a child as persistently absent and you have already decided that the problem is presence. Ask instead whether that child is meaningfully engaged in learning, and you begin somewhere entirely different, with a different child in front of you, and a different remedy in mind.

Words are never neutral. They quietly define the systems we build.

There was a recurring recognition, too, that our educational language is struggling to keep pace with reality. Adults work flexibly. Universities blend the physical and the digital. Healthcare combines face-to-face and remote care. Yet education still tends to frame flexibility as an exception rather than a legitimate design choice.

For many young people; neurodivergent, living with chronic illness, navigating school-based anxiety, or balancing elite sport, caring responsibilities or international lives - that framing carries a real cost. The question is no longer whether flexible education exists. It does. The question is how to do it well.

One question kept returning throughout the day: should the sector establish an Online Schools Association?

I'm going to leave that one open, deliberately. Not because I'm undecided, but because I think it arrives slightly before its moment. Communities are not built by constitutions. They are built by conversations: by generous sharing, by evidence offered freely, by curiosity replacing competition, by practitioners willing to say we tried this, here's what worked, here's what didn't. The structure, when it comes, should harden around something that already exists, not stand in for it.

So I hope those conversations continue, and I suspect the association will look after itself once there is enough trust to hold it up.

Trust, more than technology and more than any governing body, is the real infrastructure of online education.

Looking around the room, I realised we are no longer simply building online schools. We are building a new educational field. Like any emerging field, it is still finding its language. Different organisations bring different strengths: examination pathways, therapeutic support, international provision, flexible and alternative learning. That diversity isn't a weakness. It's how healthy ecosystems evolve. No single school will solve every challenge; together, we might begin to solve problems none of us could solve alone.

That is the conversation I hope to continue here, not because online schools hold every answer (they don't), nor because traditional schools have failed (many change children's lives every day), but because the future of education is becoming more flexible, more relational and more personal than the systems we inherited. The most exciting question is no longer whether that future is possible. It is how we choose to build it. Together.

A conversation, not a conclusion.

If you were at Pearson Online Schools Day, I'd love to know what stayed with you. If you weren't, I'd love to know what questions you think our sector should be asking next. Because the most important thing I brought home wasn't a slide deck. It was a renewed sense that the future of education won't be written by any single school, organisation or platform. It will be written by a field slowly learning to trust itself.
