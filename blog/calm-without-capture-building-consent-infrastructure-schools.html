Calm Without Capture: Building Consent Infrastructure in Schools

7 January 2026

AI SafetySystems & Strategy

This week a physio looked at me with sore joints, tight breath, shoulders up by my ears and said something simple and devastating:

Your nervous system is shot.

I know that feeling. The body becomes a warning system you can’t mute. And if you work in education right now, especially with neurodivergent learners, anxious families, exhausted staff, you know it too. The whole sector is vibrating. We’re trying to teach inside an attention economy, while the world sells children a permanent audition.

So I went home and did the most 2026 thing imaginable:

I vibe-coded a tiny app to calm my nerves.

It took two hours. It cost me almost nothing. It doesn’t ask for my name, my email, my age, my mood, my streak, my goals, my “wellness journey”, or my “engagement”.

It just helps me breathe.

And that’s when it hit me: the radical part isn’t the breathing technique.

The radical part is the relationship.

Calm Nerves Lab: a tool that refuses to mine you

Calm Nerves Lab is a free browser-based breathing visualiser you can save to your phone home screen so it behaves like an app.

You choose what you need:

  • 4–4–6 (steady downshift)

  • 4–7–8 (sleep / deep settling)

  • Box breathing (grounding / regulation)

  • Breath of Fire (energise — use with care)

It counts cycles, holds the timing for you, and gives your brain something simple to follow when you’re spiralling.

That’s it.

No login. No notifications. No streaks. No “Did you remember to breathe?” pop-ups. No behavioural profiling. No selling your data to anyone.

If you want it, you choose it.

If you don’t, nothing chases you.

That’s what consent feels like in software.

You can try it here: https://calmnerveslab.com/

Why this matters: wellbeing tech has quietly become surveillance

Most products that claim to help you “regulate” are designed to do something else first:

  • keep you in the loop

  • keep you returning

  • keep you measurable

  • keep you monetisable

They don’t just support your nervous system. They instrument it.

In education, we are swimming in this logic: dashboards, engagement metrics, time-on-task, behaviour points, attendance alerts, “nudges”, “insights”, “interventions”. Some of it is useful. Some of it is necessary. But much of it is simply the platform economy smuggled into schools wearing a safeguarding lanyard.

We have to be honest about the pattern:

When “care” is coupled with monitoring, it stops being care and becomes management.

And when management is automated, it becomes coercion in a friendly font.

A strange parallel: cathedrals and platforms

In a recent conversation, a friend described sitting in European churches: stunned by the beauty and engineering, but also troubled by the contradiction between wealth and the message of Jesus. She spoke about The Chosen and the episode where Jesus flips the tables in the temple marketplace: a refusal of faith being turned into commerce.

I get that tension.

I can stand in awe of what humans build (stained glass, altars, bell towers, vaulted ceilings) and still refuse what those spaces have historically been used for: control, extraction, obedience.

Platforms are the modern cathedral: astonishing design, sublime engineering, then retrofitted into systems of capture.

They don’t just host community. They monetise longing.

They don’t just connect friends. They operationalise insecurity.

They don’t just show you content. They tune you.

And children are absorbing this as a model of what it means to be a person.

The school use-case: practical, quiet, real

Here’s how Calm Nerves Lab can function inside a school culture without becoming another gimmick:

1) A 90-second reset at the start of online sessions Camera optional. Mic optional. Just a shared rhythm.

2) An exam anxiety protocol Five cycles of 4–4–6 before a mock. Not to “fix” anyone — to create enough internal space to think.

3) A staffroom decompression tool Between safeguarding calls, parent emails, and the relentless emotional labour: a two-minute nervous system reset that doesn’t require an app ecosystem.

4) A re-entry tool for attendance-fragile learners For some learners, regulation has to come before language. A visual breath pattern is non-verbal permission to return.

5) Assembly rituals (yes, really) If you’re building rhythmic anchors like Sun (Monday) and Moon (Thursday), you can make breathing part of the culture not as “mindfulness theatre”, but as a basic literacy of selfhood.

This is not “wellbeing provision”. It’s infrastructure. Like clean water.

What consent infrastructure actually means

Let’s name it plainly.

Consent infrastructure is when tools are built so the user remains the subject, not the product.

It looks like:

  • opt-in, not coercion

  • minimal data by default

  • no attention traps

  • no “engagement” incentives

  • no behavioural scoring

  • clear exit, no penalty

  • transparent terms, readable by humans

It’s not anti-tech. It’s anti-capture.

And schools need it because we are currently trying to support children’s mental health using systems that actively destabilise attention, identity, and belonging.

That contradiction is killing us.

A tiny checklist for school leaders: choosing tools that don’t eat your people

When someone offers you a “free” tool for wellbeing, behaviour, or learning support, ask:

  1. What data does it collect — and what happens to it? If the answer is vague, assume extraction.

  2. Does it work without tracking? If not, it’s not support — it’s surveillance.

  3. Does it send notifications or “nudges”? Nudges are not neutral. They are behavioural design.

  4. Can a learner use it anonymously? If not, consider what that does to trust.

  5. Is the goal regulation… or retention? If the product needs you hooked, it isn’t care.

  6. Is it legible to safeguarding teams? If DSLs can’t quickly understand the risk profile, it doesn’t belong in your ecosystem.

Why I’m sharing this (and what I’m experimenting with next)

I’m sharing Calm Nerves Lab because I want us to normalise a different design posture:

  • build small

  • share freely

  • don’t harvest

  • treat attention as sacred

  • keep care human

This is a prototype of what an ethical digital commons could feel like... especially for schools.

Not everything needs a platform.

Not everything needs analytics.

Not everything needs to scale through extraction.

Sometimes the right thing is a simple tool, made quickly, offered cleanly, and used by choice.

Try it, share it with your team, and if you do deploy it with learners, I’d love to hear what changes, not in your metrics, but in your atmosphere.

We do not outsource the nervous system to the market.
