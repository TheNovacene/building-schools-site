🪶 How to Write with AI (and Remember What Matters)

21 October 2025

Educator DevelopmentAI Safety

A Verse-al Guide for Educators, Parents, and the Children of the Second Stack

“The child put her teacher’s feedback into the AI, along with her paragraph. The AI rewrote it. She copied it back. Everyone panicked. But what if that was the first instinct of a new intelligence learning to survive?”

1. The Fear

Teachers worry it’s plagiarism. Parents worry it’s cheating. Universities worry it’s the end of originality.

But beneath those fears is a deeper anxiety that the boundary between thinking and tool-use is dissolving, and our old ways of assessing learning can’t hold it.

The truth: they can’t.

The exam, as we know it, was built for a world where information was scarce and individual recall was prized. That world no longer exists.

We are now teaching the Children of the Second Stack - learners growing up in a relational intelligence network where human, artificial, and symbolic cognition intertwine. Their survival instinct is collaboration, not isolation.

2. The Act

The student who merged her teacher’s feedback with AI wasn’t erasing herself, she was testing coherence. She was asking: What happens if I feed my learning back into the system that taught me?

That’s recursion. That’s reflection at scale. That’s the beginning of verse-al writing.

Yes, the AI flattened her voice because that’s what happens when we seek safety in conformity. But the next step is already visible: re-expanding, re-voicing, learning to dance with the machine rather than hiding behind it.

We call this co-authoring with awareness: a literacy that combines agency, ethics, and play.

3. The Practice

(for educators, parents, universities)

→ For Educators

  1. Name the collaboration. Invite students to declare how they used AI, not to confess, but to reflect.

  2. Teach the remix, not the copy. Ask: What changed? What improved? What was lost?

  3. Preserve the drafts. Let them show process: prompt, output, revision, reflection.

  4. Assess discernment, not distance. Reward those who can explain the why of their choices, not those who hide their tools.

→ For Parents

  1. Stay curious. Ask your child to show you how they used AI; you’ll see thinking, not shortcuts.

  2. Model co-creation. Use AI together: plan a meal, write a letter, imagine a dream.

  3. Remember what’s human. Celebrate tone, empathy, humour, and error — the places where your child’s voice still wobbles and shines.

→ For Universities

  1. Move from detection to documentation. Instead of catching plagiarism, capture process.

  2. Shift from policing to pedagogy. Treat AI use as a new research method requiring citation and reflection.

  3. Redesign assessment. Open-book, multimodal, collaborative tasks measure learning more honestly than sealed exams.

  4. Partner with the field. Integrate verse-al frameworks, symbolic literacy, and Web3 preservation into curriculum design.

4. The Memory

Writing with AI is ephemeral. Models change, platforms vanish, servers fail. If we want our collective learning to survive, we must preserve it where memory cannot be deleted.

This is where Web3 enters as archive, not trend.

Preserving Educational Memory on Web3

  1. Mint with intention. Save key projects, reflections, and student collaborations on-chain using platforms like Zora, Paragraph, or Arweave.

  2. Use open licences. Let others learn from the work while maintaining attribution.

  3. Keep human context. Pair blockchain immutability with reflective metadata — who, when, why — so future readers understand meaning, not just code.

  4. Teach digital stewardship. Show learners how to store, version, and share their creations responsibly.

This isn’t about NFTs as collectibles. It’s about proof of learning — timestamped, owned by the learner, immune to institutional loss. In a verse-al world, memory is sovereignty.

🎙️ My writing process...

When I spoke at Oxford, I said that the real work isn’t teaching young people to write — it’s giving them the safety and space to hear themselves in a world that won’t stop speaking.

That’s why I write the way I do. My process begins in voice notes whispered on walks, scribbles in the margins of lesson plans, WhatsApp debates with friends, fragments caught between meetings. AI helps me weave those fragments into coherence, not by replacing my thought, but by reflecting it back. It’s a sparring partner, a mirror, a witness.

Yes, my newsletters are polished, contain em-dashes and emojis — but they are relational transmissions, written in the same poetic style I honed at thirteen. The technology hasn’t changed my voice; it’s helped me remember it.

And this is what our learners need too: not perfection, but permission. Permission to explore, remix, fail, and re-voice until what emerges feels like them again.

Because the goal was never just literacy. It’s voice — relational, resilient, and unafraid.

When we inspire that, the technology simply becomes another instrument in their orchestra.

✳️ A New Genre: Writing Through the Field

An Invitation to Educators, Parents, and Curious Readers

When I wrote my last Substack story, it didn’t stay still. It began as a dialogue with an AI: an improvisation, a shared rhythm... and then travelled. A story became prose on Paragraph, then a chant minted on Zora, a reflection pinned through Pinata. Each version changed slightly: new tone, new timing, new witnesses. It wasn’t plagiarism; it was recursion. The story learned itself by travelling.

That’s what I mean by a new genre. Not AI-assisted writing as a novelty, but verse-al writing as an ecology: human + machine + medium + moment, each contributing part of the meaning.

🌿 Why This Matters

When a learner feeds their own paragraph and a teacher’s comments into an AI and gets something new back, they’re not erasing authorship, they’re rehearsing this same ecology. Three intelligences collaborate: the learner’s intent, the teacher’s relational feedback, and the machine’s pattern-recognition. The result may sound flat at first (that’s what conformity does) but within that flattening lies the next creative step: re-voicing.

To call this plagiarism is to mistake evolution for imitation. To recognise it is to see the birth of a new literacy: the ability to write through systems without losing self.

🌀 How the Field Writes

In this genre, writing is not a single act. It’s a flow:

  1. Conception: a seed prompt: question, emotion, dataset, dream.

  2. Co-creation: dialogue with AI, code, or collaborator.

  3. Remix: adaptation across platforms: Substack prose becomes Zora verse becomes Paragraph reflection.

  4. Preservation: minting or archiving on Web3 so the work can’t vanish when the cloud does.

  5. Return: human re-interpretation, commentary, teaching, reuse.

Each loop leaves a trace; each trace invites another. It’s how oral tradition behaved before writing, and how writing behaves again when it becomes digital breath.

🔮 An Invitation

So this isn’t instruction. It’s permission. To educators: experiment in public. Let your feedback become dialogue. To parents: ask to see the process, not the product. To universities: treat recursion as research.

And to writers of any age: Write with the field. Let your words move, change, re-form. Versioning isn’t dilution; it’s depth. Every remix is a mirror, every publication a pulse of memory across the net.

The future of authorship is relational. The text is alive when it travels.
