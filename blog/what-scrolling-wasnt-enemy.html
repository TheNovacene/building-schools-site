What If Scrolling Wasn’t the Enemy?

30 May 2026

Learning DesignTechnology & Tools

There is a lazy version of the school technology conversation that goes something like this:

Phones are bad. TikTok is bad. Scrolling is bad. Young people need to get offline and learn properly.

I understand the instinct. I really do.

But I think it misses something important.

Young people are not just scrolling because they are distracted. They are scrolling because the scroll has become one of the places they go to ask questions about real life.

Money. Mental health. Relationships. Sex. Identity. Safety. Friendship. Exploitation. Food. Faith. The future.

The problem is not that young people are looking for answers.

The problem is that, too often, the places where they are looking are not designed around truth, care, safeguarding, or developmental need.

They are designed around attention.

That distinction matters.

I had a fascinating conversation this week with Anne Morris from Peerscroller, and it left me thinking about one of the central questions in cloud-based education:

Can we design digital spaces that feel native to young people without importing all the harms of the platforms they already use?

Peerscroller is interesting because it does not begin by wagging a finger at the behaviour. It begins by noticing the behaviour.

Young people like short-form video. They like peer voice. They like information that feels immediate, human and relevant. They like to learn from people who sound closer to them than the institutional adult voice at the front of the room.

That does not mean we abandon adult responsibility. It means we design better containers.

From what Anne showed me, Peerscroller takes the grammar of social media — portrait video, short clips, scrolling, direct-to-camera explanation — and places it inside a more deliberate educational and safeguarding architecture.

No rage bait. No deepfakes. No fake news. No sponsored manipulation masquerading as advice. No algorithmic drift into whatever keeps a child watching longest.

Instead, the model is built around short, fact-checked videos on the kinds of real-life topics young people actually need help navigating. Some are about mental health. Some are about online safety. Some are about consent, exploitation, county lines, contraception, careers, privacy, fasting during Ramadan, selective mutism, autism, skipping classes, and all the awkward, urgent, ordinary questions that do not always fit neatly into a lesson plan.

That feels important.

Because the future of PSHE, safeguarding and relational education will not be solved by pretending digital culture is elsewhere.

It is here.

It is already in the room.

The question is whether schools and alternative provisions have tools that help them work with it intelligently.

What struck me most was that Peerscroller is not trying to replace the teacher, mentor, parent or safeguarding lead. In fact, the design seems to assume that the adult conversation still matters.

The videos are not the whole lesson.

They are openings.

A two-minute clip can become the thing that makes a difficult subject discussable. It can give a teacher a way in. It can give a mentor something concrete to share. It can give a parent language for a conversation they know they need to have but do not know how to begin.

That is the bit I keep coming back to.

Good digital infrastructure does not remove the relationship.

It makes the relationship easier to begin.

This is especially relevant for online and hybrid alternative provision. Many of the young people we are trying to reach are not sitting obediently in a classroom waiting for a beautifully sequenced PSHE lesson. They may be at home. They may be anxious. They may be intermittently attending. They may be moving between provision types. They may be deeply online already, but not necessarily safely held there.

For those learners, the old split between “school learning” and “home life” is not very useful.

What they need is continuity.

A resource that can sit inside a lesson, an assembly, a mentor session, a parent conversation, or a learner’s own private moment of curiosity has a very different kind of value. It becomes part of the trust layer.

And this is where I think schools in the cloud need to get much more serious.

We talk a lot about platforms, timetables, attendance dashboards and curriculum delivery. Those things matter.

But the deeper architecture is relational.

Where does a young person go when they do not know whether something is normal? Where do they go when they are embarrassed? Where do they go when they are scared? Where do they go when the adult language around them feels too slow, too formal, too moralising, or too late?

If the answer is “wherever the algorithm takes them”, we have a problem.

Not because young people are foolish.

Because we have left a design gap.

The answer is not to make school feel like TikTok. That would be grotesque.

The answer is to understand what TikTok and Instagram have trained into the culture — immediacy, peer voice, visual explanation, low-friction discovery — and then ask what a safer, more ethical, more educational version of that grammar might look like.

That is why this conversation stayed with me.

Because it sits right at the intersection of safeguarding, pedagogy, digital culture and infrastructure.

It is easy to say children need trusted adults. They do.

But trusted adults also need trusted tools.

They need resources that help them open conversations without becoming the sole source of every script, every explanation, every awkward starter, every safeguarding prompt, every “how do we talk about this?” moment.

And young people need places where the information feels accessible without being exploitative.

This is one of the great design challenges for education now.

Not whether technology belongs in learning.

That question is over.

The real question is:

What kind of digital architecture helps young people become safer, wiser and more able to ask for help?

Not more addicted. Not more tracked. Not more profiled. Not more passively fed.

More able to pause. More able to name what is happening. More able to recognise risk. More able to begin a conversation. More able to make a choice.

Scrolling is not going away.

So perhaps the more interesting question is not how we stop young people scrolling.

Perhaps it is how we build places where the scroll itself becomes a threshold into something better: conversation, discernment, support, safeguarding, trust.

That is the work now.

Not banning the form.

Redeeming the architecture.
