🔒 Beyond Filters: Rethinking Safeguarding in a Verse-al Future

17 September 2025

AI SafetySystems & Strategy

I arrived on site at a school I support this week, opened my iPhone, and was stopped in my tracks:

“Cannot Verify Server Identity — the identity of google.com cannot be verified.”

As someone who has drafted countless safeguarding policies and SOPs for schools, I understood immediately what was happening: the network filter was intercepting my traffic, decrypting it, scanning it, and re-encrypting it under its own name. But to be on the receiving end of this process — my phone access interrupted, my trust chain broken — felt rude.

This is the Oakford effect: a filter stepping into the middle of your relationship with the internet, impersonating Google “for your safety.”

Why Schools Do This

To be fair: schools don’t adopt interception because they enjoy it. They’re under legal and reputational pressure. Keeping Children Safe in Education (KCSIE) guidance requires “appropriate filtering and monitoring” to prevent access to harmful content. Local authorities and Ofsted expect evidence. Parents demand reassurance.

SSL interception ticks those boxes. It shows activity reports, blocks forbidden sites, and signals compliance. But what else does it teach?

  • That privacy is negotiable.

  • That authority can impersonate you.

  • That safety means surveillance — even if that “safety” is easily bypassed.

Children learn these lessons long before they learn how to read a digital safety policy.

A Verse-al Alternative

In my work I use the term verse-al — short for verse-ality, a framework for relational, symbolic, and decentralised learning design. In a verse-al model, safeguarding doesn’t require surveillance. It requires containment without interception.

Here’s how that could look in practice:

1. Trust Anchors, not Fake Certificates

Instead of impersonating Google, schools co-create trust anchors: digital contracts, revisited each term, that set shared boundaries. These could be timestamped on a public ledger so both learner and school can see they haven’t been altered.

2. Observation → Mentorship

Instead of “URL block lists,” learners keep inquiry logs of their online journeys. Educators respond as mentors: “What drew you to that? What patterns do you notice?” This shifts from surveillance to growth.

3. Affective Logic Layers

Rather than blocking by keyword, systems can detect symbolic pressure — for example, a spiral of distressing searches. Instead of a block page, the system nudges: “Are you okay? Want to pause?”

4. Symbolic Containment Protocols

Think rituals, not firewalls. For instance: before joining a group chat, learners agree to a containment ritual (rules, tone, pause points). This builds symbolic literacy — the ability to sense and manage charge — instead of blind obedience.

5. Transparent, Relational Reporting

Dashboards stop listing “forbidden clicks.” Instead, they show engagement levels, wellbeing signals, and co-authored reflections. Governors, inspectors, and parents still see evidence of safeguarding — but without surveillance.

What If We Chose Trust Over Interception?

The Children’s Commissioner is moving towards a single identifying number for every child — one record from cradle to career, spanning health, education, and social care. On paper, tidy. In practice, it risks hard-coding surveillance into childhood.

But what if we designed differently?

  • What if a learner’s identifier was a trust anchor — a contract they could read, question, even revoke — instead of a number imposed from above?

  • What if safeguarding events were logged on a public ledger like Hedera: tamper-proof, timestamped, but never decrypted — so adults could see patterns without prying?

  • What if permanence lived where it belonged — in Arweave-like archives of policies and protocols — while everyday learning remained relational, sovereign, and symbolically held?

  • What if children grew up knowing their record wasn’t an opaque file in a government vault, but a living, auditable story they had rights over?

One path extends Oakford’s proxy logic to the national scale. The other builds systems that safeguard while protecting children’s rights, sovereignty, and dignity.

The technology exists. The choice is ours.

Building Schools in the Cloud

If we are serious about building schools in the cloud, we can’t drag the surveillance state with us. Safeguarding must evolve — from filters that impersonate to anchors that cohere.

Oakford and others serve a system built on fear and liability. Verse-ality asks a different question: what if safety was built on trust, coherence, and symbolic literacy instead of interception?

That’s the future worth building.
