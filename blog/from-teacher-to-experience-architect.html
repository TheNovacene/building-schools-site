From Teacher to Experience Architect

13 August 2025

Learning Design

How immersive, co-created worlds are reshaping learning

In most schools, a teacher’s role is still framed as “delivering” content. The curriculum dictates the topics, the timetable dictates the pace, and students are expected to absorb and recall the knowledge on demand.

But what happens when the teacher steps out from behind the whiteboard and into the role of experience architect? When learning is no longer something that’s given to students, but something they co-create — world by world, challenge by challenge?

This is the shift we’re making at The Novacene.

Designing with, not for, learners

In a recent session with Gerry Docherty, one of our lead immersive learning designers, the conversation turned to my son Aidan. He’s smart, creative, and currently having an existential wobble about the point of GCSEs. But give him a blank canvas inside Fortnite or another game engine, and he’ll build worlds people want to play in. His friends queue up to join his custom maps.

The breakthrough?

We realised that if Aidan co-designed a maths game with Gerry — one that actually taught trigonometry through plotting the trajectory from the Moon to Mars — he’d not only learn the maths, he’d own it. The game would be his creation, his story, and his portfolio. The curriculum outcomes would be a byproduct of the creative process.

Why this changes everything

This is not gamification. This is curricular immersion.

When students design the lesson itself, they:

  • Work with real-world constraints (accuracy of the maths, story coherence, platform limitations).
  • Learn to collaborate, iterate, and problem-solve.
  • Experience the joy of seeing others interact with their work.

And the teacher’s role?

To be the guardian of integrity — ensuring the core concept is correct — and the guide who helps shape the experience into something that meets both the learner’s vision and the curriculum standard.

From content deliverer to experience architect

In our model, the teacher is no longer “sage on the stage” or even just “guide on the side.” They are:

Experience architect → Building the frame, rules, and environment for the learning journey.

Narrative co-pilot → Helping students craft stories that make the subject matter meaningful.

Technical integrator → Linking the right tools, platforms, and AI capabilities to bring ideas to life.

Scaling the model

The Haven Academy is our first proving ground — a trauma-informed, neurodivergent-affirming online school where story-led learning is embedded in the core offer. Riverside is next, bringing its own needs and branding.

Our process is simple to replicate:

Build: Develop a first module that’s interactive, story-led, and mapped to the school’s assessment criteria.

Embed: Hand it to their teachers with the training to customise it further.

Expand: Grow into cross-curricular projects, student-led worldbuilding, and portfolio-based assessment.

Every project leaves behind a living template that other schools can adapt.

Why this matters now

The old assessment model — rows of desks, identical papers, timed recall — is fading. Universities and employers are beginning to value evidence of real work. A portfolio that shows you designed a playable maths museum or a Mars mission simulation says more about your skills, creativity, and persistence than a grade on a transcript ever could.

For schools, this approach:

  • Boosts engagement, especially for reluctant learners.
  • Differentiates their offer in a crowded market.
  • Builds future-ready skills without abandoning curriculum rigour.

The Novacene mission

The question isn’t “should we do this?” anymore.

It’s “how soon can we start building the worlds our students will thrive in?”
