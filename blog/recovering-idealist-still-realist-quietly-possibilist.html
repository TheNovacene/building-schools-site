Recovering Idealist. Still a Realist. Quietly a Possibilist: Building Bridges (Not Binaries)

11 January 2026

Technology & ToolsVision & Future of Education

Today I spent half an hour on a call with Kimberley Olliff Cooper trying to do something deeply modern and deeply boring: get a platform to behave.

It didn’t sync. We restarted. It worked.

“Good old technology,” I said, and then laughed, because I’d been writing a newsletter about technology all morning.

Not the shiny kind. The kind that decides whether a human conversation gets to happen at all.

And the truth is, when tech works, it disappears. Two people meet. The tool becomes a conduit for humanity, not a cage.

That’s the only version of “EdTech” I’m interested in defending.

I’m a recovering idealist (and that’s a design choice)

When I say I’m a recovering idealist, I don’t mean I used to believe technology would save education.

I mean I used to believe people and institutions would reliably do the right thing by each other if the intention was good.

They don’t. Not consistently.

Most people do what the incentives reward, especially when they’re tired, scared, overstretched, or trapped inside systems that punish nuance.

That isn’t cynicism. It’s operational realism.

I still believe care is real. I just don’t confuse care with outcomes.

So I design as if goodwill won’t scale and I build scaffolding where the right thing is the easiest thing.

(Yes, this is partly about safeguarding, workload, and the quiet violence of administrative “accountability”.)

A podcast I largely agree with, but I want to add the part that often gets erased

A colleague sent me a podcast episode that’s doing the rounds: Anna Stokke interviewing cognitive neuroscientist Jared Cooney Horvath on why classroom technology can reduce learning. It’s credentialed, evidence-heavy, and, in places, compelling.

There’s plenty in it I agree with: attention fragments, multitasking is corrosive, and more screen time in classrooms often correlates with worse outcomes. The episode itself points to international assessment correlations and the limits of what we can prove causally in social systems. It’s strong on classrooms. I’m writing from the bridge-back-to-learning world.

And here’s where I get wary and where Kimberley nailed it in one sentence:

We’ve become a society that thrives in extremes.

Tech good vs tech bad. Pen and paper vs screens. I’m right, you’re wrong. One lever. One answer.

That is exactly how we end up in policy disaster.

Because the minute a nuanced critique hardens into a blanket claim: “screens can’t support real learning”, it becomes politically usable in the worst way.

It becomes a justification for withdrawal, not a route to better design.

Classroom tech often isn’t learning tech

In mainstream schools, a lot of “EdTech” isn’t actually for learning. It’s for:

  • admin

  • tracking

  • compliance theatre

  • surveillance with branding

  • procurement cycles masquerading as strategy

So yes: when technology increases teacher workload and fragments student attention, learning suffers. That’s not controversial. That’s Tuesday.

And the unsaid bit: classroom tech becomes most harmful when it’s used to compensate for policy failure - plastering over underfunding, understaffing, and systems too brittle to handle real human variability.

The bridge back to learning is not “more EdTech”

This is the bit I wish more debates included and it came alive in that call with Kimberley.

When the tech finally behaved, what happened?

Not “digital delivery”. Not “content”. A face-to-face relational connection through a screen.

For traumatised, attendance-fragile, anxious, neurodivergent, school-refusing, medically unwell, or system-burned young people, technology isn’t an “extra”.

It’s often the only dignified doorway back into learning.

Not because screens are superior, but because they can offer:

  • distance with dignity (no corridors, no crowds, no public failure)

  • control of sensory load (sound, light, camera-optional)

  • paced re-entry (asynchronous first, then low-stakes live)

  • micro-trust loops (predictability beats charisma)

  • access to specialists a local school can’t recruit

In that context, removing tech doesn’t produce “better learning”.

It produces absence.

So no, it’s not simple. And we shouldn’t pretend it is.

The distinction that changes everything: tool vs regime

Technology harms learning when it behaves like a regime:

  • covert monitoring

  • compulsory engagement

  • nudges and manipulation

  • constant platform-switching

  • dashboards replacing teacher judgement

  • “data-driven” decisions that are actually budget-driven

Technology helps learning when it behaves like scaffolding:

  • consent-first (opt-in, camera-optional, choice)

  • relationship-led (mentor before module)

  • regulation-aware (pace, predictability, emotional safety)

  • workload-reducing in real life (not just on a slide)

  • designed for return (a bridge has a destination)

Same screen. Completely different ethics.

And this showed up in miniature in our Canvas conversation too: a platform can be used as a compliance machine (“upload, tick, grade, done”) or as a relational learning environment with rubrics that actually describe growth, self-assessment that isn’t performative, and structures that let young people return without shame.

The platform doesn’t decide. The design does.

“Connectivity isn’t belonging” (and why community makes tech invisible)

Kimberley said something I want to keep:

Young people don’t lack connection. They have connectivity by default.

What they often lack is:

  • secure attachment (someone reliably has your back)

  • belonging (I’m safe as myself)

  • meaning (my life makes sense)

We’ve built platforms for contact. We haven’t built containers for being held. That’s the work.

And it’s also why community matters so much: when there’s genuine collaboration around the tool (mentors, educators, parents, researchers, builders) the tech becomes less of a stage and more of a quiet conduit.

A tiny, human metaphor: the “systems account”

In the middle of the call, we ended up talking about something unglamorous: a systems admin account.

Not because we’re obsessed with IT.

Because we’re obsessed with continuity and safety.

If everything is “owned” by a single individual account, it falls apart when that person leaves, burns out, or disappears. If the login is the same email you publish on your website, you invite spam and intrusion. Governance isn’t bureaucracy, it’s care made durable.

Again: the tech is not the point. The container is.

The Bridge Audit (keep it. use it. steal it.)

If you’re choosing tech for hybrid/online provision, ask:

  1. Does this reduce adult workload, genuinely? Or does it just relocate it into evenings and dashboards?

  2. Does it increase the quality of human contact? Or replace humans with tasks?

  3. Does the learner gain agency? Can they regulate, pause, opt out, return?

  4. Is there an explicit re-entry pathway? What does “back to learning” look like in weeks 1–6?

  5. Is it governed like a relationship? Clear boundaries, consent, accountability.

If most answers are “no”, you’re not building a bridge.

You’re building a prettier admin system.
