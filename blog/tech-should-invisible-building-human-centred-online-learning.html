Tech Should Be Invisible: Building Human-Centred Online Learning

31 August 2025

Learning DesignTechnology & Tools

Think of a lesson like looking down a microscope. When the light is right and the focus is set, the glass disappears and the living thing comes into view. Online learning should feel the same.

“My goal is to make the tech obsolete so that the only thing left in an online school is the human that you see.” Kirstin S.

Below are the hidden problems most schools bump into online — and the patterns we use to solve them.

1) The Attention Tax

The problem Learners and families are asked to juggle logins, dashboards and notifications. It feels like squinting through glare: you can’t see the specimen for the reflections.

How it shows up Missed tasks, late work, “I never saw that message”, and adults spending evenings hunting through five inboxes.

Our pattern (the fix) We pick one surface (Canvas), set notifications that reach a human, and remove everything that doesn’t serve the relationship. We publish a simple route map so everyone knows where to click and why.

Why it matters When the optics are clean, attention lands where it should: on the learner.

“Press the button and it just goes to the right people, so no one falls through.”

2) Safeguarding in the Dark

The problem Online, a concern can vanish into a platform: a learner says something, a form is filled, and then… nothing. No one is sure who saw it, what was recorded, or what happened next.

How it feels Uncertain. People assume “the system” will notify someone. It often doesn’t — or it notifies the wrong someone, with too little detail to act.

Our pattern (the fix) We run two tracks that meet:

  • Track A — Anonymous signal (for learners/families): a simple web form that routes an immediate alert to the DSL/deputies.

  • Track B — Formal record (for staff): a full, KCSIE-aligned safeguarding entry held against the learner’s profile (role-restricted).

What the formal record must include

  • Full name, date of birth, and year group of the child/young person

  • Full name and job title of the person writing the record

  • Date and time of writing, and when the incident/concern happened/began

  • Clear details of the incident/concern: what raised concern; who you spoke to and what was said

  • Actions taken and outcomes to date

  • The extent and nature of any involvement by other professionals (including colleagues), children or adults — with full details, not initials (redact later only if necessary when sharing)

  • Date and author’s signature at the end of the record (electronic signature acceptable)

Where it lives: The master record is captured on the learner’s profile under a role-restricted safeguarding section. Anonymous submissions (Track A) trigger the alert; the DSL opens/creates the formal entry (Track B) and completes the checklist above so the audit trail is whole.

The visible light path (what happens, every time):

  1. Signal received → instant alert to DSL/deputies (email/Slack).

  2. Record created/updated fields completed.

  3. Triage & actions logged (who did what, when).

  4. Follow-up recorded (including contact with family/professionals).

  5. Closure & review noted; retention handled per policy.

Why this matters It turns a whisper into a routable, accountable beam — from first report to action, without glare or gaps.

Staff mantra: If in doubt, log it. The DSL would rather close a benign record than miss a serious one.

3) Fit Before Features

The problem Great classroom teachers assume they’ll be great online. Parents assume their child will thrive, then discover the rhythm is very different.

How it shows up Rigid availability (“I don’t answer after three”), frustrated families, learners labelled “hard to reach”.

Our pattern (the fix) Three short profiles — learner, parent, teacher — that act like the first, low-power scan at 10×. We agree contact rhythms that suit real lives (time zones, ND energy), and we escalate clearly when something’s off.

“A lot of people think 25 years in a classroom = ready for online — sometimes they’re the worst.” — Mandy Perry

Result Fewer mismatches, more trust, quicker support.

4) Running a School Without a Server

The problem Many online provisions start with no central IT. Devices go out with full admin rights; games and VPNs creep in; backups are “somewhere”.

How it feels You’re the head, the DSL, and the tech admin — and the course vanishes the night before term.

Our pattern (the fix) A device build sheet (accounts, updates, minimal controls), versioned courses, a dull but tight GDPR map, and a simple backup/restore routine anyone can follow.

Result Fewer fires. When something breaks, you recover — quickly and cleanly.

5) AI That You Can Mark

The problem “AI” is sold as chatty magic. Teachers can’t assess magic.

What we do instead We specify instruments learners can use — then ask AI to draft the code. Gerry’s virtual microscope is the model: coarse/fine focus, lamp, objectives, stage controls and a Capture PNG button. Learners submit a screenshot and a short comparison.

“I just said, Can you build me a microscope? — ‘Oh yeah, okay then.’ Give me the code.” — Gerry Docherty

Why it works It creates evidence you can moderate: an image tied to choices (e.g., “Cheek cells, 40×, fine 62%, light 45%”) plus a reflection in scientific language. It’s not gee-whizz; it’s assessable learning.
